\section{Background}
This chapter discusses the fundamentals of Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and its applications to cardiovascular imaging, particularly the use of phase contrast sequences. Additionally, techniques for improving and accelerating image acquisition are discussed.
\section{Magnetic Resonance Imaging}
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a noninvasive imaging technique that produces detailed images of internal structures by exploiting the magnetic properties of atomic nuclei, particularly hydrogen nuclei (protons) in water and fat. In the absence of an external magnetic field, these hydrogen nuclei have randomly oriented spins but when a magnetic field $B_0$ is applied, the spins partially align with or against the field. This alignment creates a small net magnetization in the direction of the field and causes the spin vectors to precess around the direction of $B_0$ at the Larmor frequency $w_0=\gamma B_0$.

To generate a measurable signal, MRI systems apply a radiofrequency (RF) pulse at the Larmor frequency which perturbs the net magnetization and tips it away from the direction of the $B_0$ field. After excitation, the spins begin to precess coherently in the transverse plane which induces a voltage in receiver coils that can be readout as the MR signal. After the RF pulse ends, the magnetization vector $M_0$ attempts to return to equilibrium through two methods: longitudinal relaxation where the parallel component of $M_0$, $M_z$, recovers according to the equation $M_z(t)=M_0(1-e^\frac{-t}{T_1})$ and transverse decay where the perpendicular component of $M_0$, $M_{xy}$, decays

\subsection{Radial Acquisitions}
Radial MRI is a non-Cartesian sampling strategy in which k-space is traversed along a series of spokes that pass through its center, rather than along the rectilinear grid used in conventional Cartesian imaging. Each spoke represents a projection of the object at a different angle, and successive spokes are rotated to achieve full k-space coverage. Because the center of k-space is sampled repeatedly, radial acquisitions inherently emphasize low spatial frequency information, which governs overall image contrast and signal intensity. This repeated central sampling contributes to improved robustness against inconsistencies during data acquisition.

One of the primary advantages of radial sampling is its resilience to motion. In Cartesian imaging, motion typically results in coherent ghosting artifacts along the phase-encoding direction. In contrast, motion in radial acquisitions leads to more diffuse, incoherent streaking artifacts that are often less visually disruptive. This property makes radial MRI particularly well suited for applications involving respiratory or cardiac motion, such as thoracic and abdominal imaging, or in patient populations where breath-holding is not feasible. Additionally, radial trajectories allow for flexible data grouping, enabling retrospective adjustment of temporal resolution in dynamic imaging applications.

Radial sampling also offers favorable characteristics for accelerated imaging. When undersampled, radial acquisitions produce incoherent artifacts that are more amenable to advanced reconstruction techniques such as compressed sensing. The oversampling of the k-space center further stabilizes image contrast and improves the performance of iterative reconstruction methods. Variants such as golden-angle radial sampling introduce a specific angular increment that optimizes k-space coverage for arbitrary subsets of data, allowing flexible trade-offs between spatial and temporal resolution without requiring changes to the acquisition.

Despite these advantages, radial MRI presents several technical challenges. The non-uniform sampling density—characterized by oversampling at the center of k-space and undersampling at higher spatial frequencies—requires the use of density compensation during reconstruction. Furthermore, radial data cannot be directly reconstructed using a standard fast Fourier transform and instead require interpolation (gridding) onto a Cartesian grid or the use of iterative reconstruction methods, both of which increase computational complexity. Streaking artifacts may also become prominent in cases of severe undersampling or in the presence of high-contrast structures. Nevertheless, ongoing developments in acquisition design and reconstruction algorithms continue to enhance the utility of radial MRI for a wide range of clinical and research applications.

\section{Phase Contrast MRI}
\subsection{2D Cine Imaging}
Two-dimensional phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (2D PC-MRI) is a well-established technique for the quantitative assessment of blood flow and velocity. It exploits the phase of the MR signal, rather than its magnitude, to encode motion. By applying bipolar gradient pulses, moving spins—such as flowing blood—accumulate a phase shift proportional to their velocity along the direction of the applied gradient, while stationary spins experience no net phase change. This enables direct, voxel-wise measurement of velocity within a prescribed imaging plane. The sensitivity of the measurement is controlled by the velocity encoding parameter (VENC), which must be appropriately selected to avoid phase aliasing while maintaining adequate sensitivity to flow.

In a typical 2D PC-MRI acquisition, images are acquired throughout the cardiac cycle using electrocardiographic (ECG) gating, producing time-resolved velocity data. Both magnitude and phase images are reconstructed: magnitude images provide anatomical context and are often used for segmentation, while phase images encode velocity information. By placing regions of interest (ROIs) within a vessel lumen, velocity waveforms can be extracted across time, and volumetric flow rates can be calculated by integrating velocity over the cross-sectional area. This capability makes 2D PC-MRI particularly valuable for assessing pulsatile hemodynamics in large vessels such as the aorta and pulmonary arteries.

Beyond basic flow quantification, 2D PC-MRI is widely used to derive secondary hemodynamic metrics. For example, pulse wave velocity (PWV), a surrogate marker of arterial stiffness, can be estimated by measuring the temporal delay between flow waveforms at different anatomical locations. Similarly, parameters such as peak velocity, net flow, regurgitant fraction, and wall shear stress (with additional modeling) can be derived. Compared to other modalities, 2D PC-MRI offers the advantage of providing spatially resolved, angle-independent velocity measurements without the need for acoustic windows, making it particularly useful in regions that are difficult to assess with ultrasound.

Despite these advantages, 2D PC-MRI has several limitations. Conventional acquisitions are typically performed using Cartesian sampling and often require breath-holding to minimize respiratory motion artifacts, which can be challenging for certain patient populations. The technique is also sensitive to errors arising from improper VENC selection, background phase offsets, and limited temporal resolution. Additionally, since measurements are confined to a single imaging plane, accurate slice positioning is critical and may introduce variability. 

\subsection{4D Flow Imaging}
Four-dimensional flow magnetic resonance imaging (4D flow MRI) is an extension of phase-contrast MRI that enables comprehensive, time-resolved, three-dimensional assessment of blood flow. In this technique, velocity is encoded in all three spatial directions across a volumetric imaging region and throughout the cardiac cycle, resulting in a dataset that captures both spatial and temporal variations in flow. Similar to 2D phase-contrast MRI, velocity encoding is achieved through the use of bipolar gradients, but repeated across multiple encoding directions to reconstruct full velocity vectors. Electrocardiographic (ECG) gating is typically employed to synchronize data acquisition with the cardiac cycle, allowing reconstruction of dynamic flow patterns over time.

A key advantage of 4D flow MRI is its ability to characterize complex hemodynamics that cannot be adequately captured with single-plane techniques. Because the entire volume is acquired, flow can be retrospectively analyzed at any location without the need for precise slice prescription during scanning. This enables detailed visualization of multidirectional flow phenomena such as vortices, helices, and turbulent jets, which are commonly observed in pathological conditions including valvular disease, aortic dilation, and congenital heart defects. In addition to qualitative visualization, the technique supports quantitative analysis of a wide range of hemodynamic parameters, including volumetric flow rates, peak velocities, wall shear stress, vorticity, and pulse wave velocity.

Despite its versatility, 4D flow MRI is associated with several technical challenges. The acquisition is inherently more time-intensive than 2D methods due to the need for volumetric coverage and multiple velocity encodings, which can limit its routine clinical use. Furthermore, spatial and temporal resolution are often lower than those achieved with targeted 2D acquisitions, potentially affecting the accuracy of derived measurements. The technique is also sensitive to noise, velocity aliasing, and background phase errors, necessitating careful selection of velocity encoding parameters and post-processing corrections.

Recent methodological advances have substantially improved the feasibility of 4D flow MRI\@. Acceleration strategies such as parallel imaging, compressed sensing, and non-Cartesian sampling (e.g., radial and spiral trajectories) have reduced acquisition times while preserving data quality. In addition, developments in reconstruction algorithms and automated post-processing tools have streamlined data analysis and enhanced reproducibility. As a result, 4D flow MRI is increasingly being adopted in both research and clinical settings for comprehensive evaluation of cardiovascular hemodynamics.

\section{Physiological Motion}
In common MR acquisitions, intrinsic motion of the heart due to both cardiac and respiratory motion results in blurring and/or ghosting of the heart in the phase encode directions~\cite{fengGoldenAngleRadialMRI2022}. Cardiac motion is routinely compensated for with physiological gating, which utilizes a physiological waveform such as an electrocardiogram (ECG) or photoplethysmogram (PPG) to prospectively or retrospectively segment the acquired data into distinct cardiac phases~\cite{jangDesignECGPPG2007}. Cardiac gating can be performed either prospectively, where the acquisition depends on triggers from the physiological waveform, or retrospectively, where data is acquired continuously and properly sorted during image reconstruction~\cite{heijmanComparisonProspectiveRetrospective2007}. Respiratory motion is most commonly managed via breath holding. Multiple sequential breath holds are used for longer multi-slice 2D scans with increased volumetric coverage, but breath hold inconsistencies can result in slice misalignment~\cite{chandlerCorrectionMisalignedSlices2008}. Similar to cardiac gating, respiratory motion can also be compensated for using a physiological gating signal, such as navigators or abdominal bellows~\cite{santelliRespiratoryBellowsRevisited2011}. Usually, reconstructed data is limited to a single respiratory phase (typically end-exhale)~\cite{hollandDiaphragmaticCardiacMotion1998}, but if comprehensive respiratory information is desired, specific acquisition schemes enable the reconstruction of multiple respiratory phases alongside multiple cardiac phases, leading to a 5D acquisition~\cite{fengXDGRASPGoldenangleRadial2016,schraubenRespiratoryinducedVenousBlood2015}. 

\section{Constrained Reconstruction}
Three-dimensional radial imaging results in motion artifacts spreading across multiple dimensions~\cite{fengGoldenAngleRadialMRI2022,iiiAdvantagesRetrospectivelyGated} rather than prominent ghosting artifacts in the phase-encoding directions seen in Cartesian imaging. Every radial projection passes through the center of k-space, so the low-frequency components of the image are sampled more often than the high-frequency components at the edges of k-space, which has an averaging effect on motion artifacts. Essentially, motion artifacts in a 3D radial acquisition appear as blurring and diffuse streaking artifacts, rather than visually distinct replicas. 
Radial sampling also allows for greater flexibility in how data are sorted during reconstruction compared to Cartesian imaging. If projections are acquired in pseudo-random order, as with a golden angle or bit-reversed view ordering~\cite{fengGoldenAngleRadialMRI2022,chanInfluenceRadialUndersampling2012} projections can be arbitrarily grouped into dynamic frames with reasonable coverage of k-space and reconstructed as images. This allows arbitrary gating based on physiological signals, as well as retrospective subsampling of full datasets to simulate shorter scan times or to remove projections compromised by bulk motion.
Lastly, the inherent variable-density sampling of radial acquisitions allows for high undersampling factors while preserving image content {fengGoldenAngleRadialMRI2022,chanInfluenceRadialUndersampling2012}. In a radial acquisition, undersampling artifacts appear as incoherent pseudo-noise rather than coherent replica artifacts as in Cartesian imaging. This is important for constrained reconstruction techniques, such as compressed sensing~\cite{lustigSparseMRIApplication2007} and low-rank approximations~\cite{haldarLowrankApproximationsDynamic2011, trzaskoExploitingLocalLowrank2013}, which exploit sparse datasets to recover high-quality images. 
More explicitly, a constrained reconstruction takes the form of the inverse problem 

\begin{equation}
    \min_{m} \; \left\| F \cdot S \cdot m - d \right\|_2^2 + \lambda \, R(m)
    \label{eq:constrained_recon}
\end{equation}

where $F$ is the nonuniform Fourier transform, $S$ are coil sensitivity maps, $m$ is the image, $d$ is the acquired k-space data, $\lambda$ is a regularization parameter, and $R$ is the enforced constraint~\cite{haldarChapter5Early2022}. The form of $R$ in Equation 1 varies depending on the type of constraint being enforced. In the case of compressed sensing, the constraint is that the data is sparse in some transformed domain and redundancies can be exploited to recover the original image, i.e., $R(m)=\|\Psi m\|_1$ where $\Psi$ is a sparsifying transform (e.g., wavelets)~\cite{lustigSparseMRIApplication2007}. For a local low-rank approximation, the assumption is that there are spatial and temporal redundancies across local patches of the dataset, which can be approximated as the product of lower-rank matrices, thereby reducing the complexity of the original matrix and approximating a solution, i.e., $R(m)=\sum_p \|\mathrm{Mat}(m_p)\|_*$ where $\mathrm{Mat}$ is a tensor unfolding operation along either a spatial or temporal dimension and $*$ is the nuclear norm~\cite{haldarLowrankApproximationsDynamic2011,leeLLORMALocalLowRank}. With either method, an iterative reconstruction can be utilized to solve Eq~\ref{eq:constrained_recon} and recover a higher-quality image from an undersampled dataset well below the Nyquist criterion, reducing the amount of data needed to be acquired and the time taken to acquire it~\cite{haldarChapter5Early2022}.
